Investment Summary

Investment Thesis T-Mobile continues to deliver the strongest fundamental growth profile among the three major U.S. wireless carriers, with Q2 2026 postpaid service revenue up 13% year-over-year and core adjusted EBITDA up 12% YoY — both "at multiples to our competition" per management's own characterization. The company has pivoted its growth strategy from raw subscriber-volume gains toward higher-value account and ARPA growth (60% of new customers now choosing premium plans, ex-M&A ARPA growth of 3.7% YoY), supporting continued double-digit service-revenue and EBITDA growth even as reported postpaid net additions decelerate (277,000 in Q2 2026, down 13% YoY, with Q3 guided even lower to ~250,000). Free cash flow generation remains industry-leading (25% margin) and management raised full-year 2026 adjusted free cash flow guidance to $18.4–$18.8 billion. The primary near-term overhang is a customer-facing "rate plan modernization" — effectively a price increase on legacy plans — that has generated measurable consumer backlash, FCC and state-attorney-general complaints, and at least one class-action lawsuit, testing whether T-Mobile's "un-carrier"/value positioning can survive its transition into the industry's largest and most profitable player. The mispricing thesis depends on whether the market is appropriately weighing durable ARPA/EBITDA growth and successful UScellular integration against the reputational and regulatory risk of behaving more like a traditional "legacy" carrier — the very dynamic Morningstar and other analysts have flagged as eroding T-Mobile's historical "maverick" positioning.

T-Mobile reported Q2 2026 total service revenue of $19.0 billion (+9% YoY) and postpaid service revenue of $15.9 billion (+13% YoY, or +12.6% per one precise source). Diluted EPS was $2.99 (+5% YoY), beating consensus of $2.59, though EPS growth lagged core EBITDA growth (+12% YoY) due to $146 million after-tax ($0.14/share) of UScellular merger-related costs and $46 million after-tax ($0.04/share) of network restructuring costs. Business Description

T-Mobile US, Inc., headquartered in Bellevue, Washington, provides wireless communications services in the United States, Puerto Rico, and the U.S. Virgin Islands under the T-Mobile and MetroPCS brands. The company offers postpaid, prepaid, and wholesale wireless voice, messaging, and data services, along with wireless devices (smartphones, wearables, tablets, home broadband gateways), equipment financing through installment plans, and device-insurance-related reinsurance. T-Mobile has built its business around a "value and network" strategy — combining a historically disruptive ("Un-carrier") pricing and marketing approach with what the company and multiple third-party sources describe as an industry-leading 5G network (record wireless Net Promoter Score of 46 in Q2 2026, multiple third-party network awards). The company has grown significantly through M&A, most notably its 2020 merger with Sprint and its more recent, smaller-scale acquisition of UScellular's wireless operations and spectrum assets, alongside a fiber broadband joint venture. Founded in 1994, T-Mobile has evolved from the historical "low-cost disruptor" position into the largest wireless carrier by several growth and profitability metrics, a transition that itself is becoming a notable strategic and reputational consideration (see Investment Risks).

Key Revenue Drivers

  • Postpaid account and ARPA growth, increasingly driven by premium-plan mix shift (~60% of new customers choosing premium plans) rather than pure subscriber-count growth
  • 5G home broadband (fixed wireless access), cited as growing strongly with fiber-equivalent speeds and a 70% bundle rate with mobile service
  • T-Life app engagement (over 30 million monthly active users, record momentum per management commentary), supporting cross-sell and retention
  • UScellular acquisition integration, adding new postpaid and prepaid customers and spectrum assets, though currently a near-term drag on reported GAAP net income due to merger-related and restructuring costs

Key Expense Drivers

  • Equipment subsidies — Q2 2026 equipment costs ($5.05 billion) exceeded equipment revenue ($3.52 billion), a widening gap reflecting aggressive device-upgrade incentives used to drive premium-plan adoption and account growth
  • UScellular merger-related and network-restructuring costs ($146 million and $46 million after-tax, respectively, in Q2 2026 alone), which are currently suppressing GAAP EPS growth relative to core EBITDA growth
  • Capital expenditures on network buildout and spectrum, with cash capex guided at approximately $10 billion for full-year 2026, alongside continued investment ahead of anticipated 2027–2028 spectrum opportunities (C-band 2.0 and 2.7 GHz)
  • Share repurchases — an incremental $2.5 billion in Q2 2026 alone (through July 17), with 253 million shares repurchased since late 2022

Industry Overview & Competitive Positioning

T-Mobile operates in the mature, oligopolistic U.S. wireless telecommunications industry alongside Verizon and AT&T, with cable companies (Comcast, Charter) increasingly competing for mobile subscribers as a fourth competitive force. T-Mobile has been the primary share gainer among the three national carriers in recent years, a trend Morningstar and other analysts expect to continue for at least the next couple of years before market share stabilizes among the three carriers as Verizon and AT&T close the network/value gap. The industry is currently navigating two notable simultaneous dynamics: continued industry consolidation (T-Mobile's own UScellular acquisition, alongside broader industry M&A activity) and a shift among all major carriers toward "value" and bundled convergence strategies (mobile plus home broadband) as a retention and ARPA-growth lever.

Competitive Advantage ("Economic Moat")

T-Mobile's moat has historically rested on superior mid-band 5G spectrum coverage and speed (330 million people covered, per one source) combined with a consumer-friendly "Un-carrier" brand reputation built over roughly a decade of disruptive, consumer-favorable pricing and policy moves. Morningstar explicitly frames the current environment as a "virtuous cycle" driven by T-Mobile's strong brand and network position. However, several sources — including Morningstar itself and TD Cowen analysts — note that this differentiation is eroding as T-Mobile becomes "very 'carrier'-like" through recent fee increases, price hikes, and the current rate-plan modernization, raising a genuine strategic question about whether the moat can persist once the company no longer occupies the low-cost disruptor position that originally built its brand loyalty.

Operational The ongoing "rate plan modernization" (retirement of legacy plans, including "Simple Choice" and "Home Internet Plus") has generated documented customer backlash, with affected customers filing complaints with the FCC and state attorneys general and pursuing at least one class-action lawsuit (Oddo v. T-Mobile); Q3 2026 postpaid net additions are explicitly guided lower (to ~250,000) due to an anticipated churn spike tied to this migration. A near-term CFO transition (Peter Osvaldik departing February 2027) and the departure of a 28-year veteran marketing/strategy executive (Mike Katz) also introduce leadership-continuity considerations.
Financial UScellular merger-related and network-restructuring costs are currently suppressing GAAP net income and EPS growth relative to underlying core EBITDA growth; equipment-cost economics show costs exceeding equipment revenue by a widening margin, reflecting continued aggressive device-subsidy spending to drive premium-plan adoption.
Regulatory A broad coalition of industry groups, public interest organizations, and a national labor union urged the FCC to closely scrutinize the T-Mobile/UScellular transaction, citing concerns about spectrum concentration among the three national carriers, potential rural tower shutdowns, and the historical pattern (following the 2020 Sprint merger) of T-Mobile raising rates roughly five years after merger-related price-hike commitments expired. U.S. senators separately urged the DOJ and FCC to scrutinize the deal on similar competition grounds. The rate-plan-modernization consumer complaints described above (FCC, state AG complaints) also represent an active regulatory-attention risk.
Legal T-Mobile faces an active class-action lawsuit (Oddo v. T-Mobile) tied to the rate-plan-modernization price increases and alleged violation of price-lock guarantees; a separate, older class action alleged that reduced competition from T-Mobile's 2020 Sprint merger caused AT&T and Verizon customers to pay billions of dollars more, illustrating a pattern of merger-related antitrust/consumer litigation risk that could recur around the UScellular transaction. T-Mobile has also faced historical shareholder-alert notices regarding potential breach-of-duty claims against officers and directors (per one source, dated 2022) and a large 2021 data-breach settlement valued at over $500 million — both older matters, but indicative of the company's litigation and cybersecurity risk profile as a large, high-profile consumer data holder. T-Mobile and Verizon have recently and mutually resolved lawsuits accusing each other of deceptive advertising, which appears to have been settled amicably.
