Business Description

GE Aerospace, the remaining entity following General Electric's historic three-way corporate breakup (GE HealthCare spun off in January 2023, GE Vernova spun off in April 2024), designs, develops, manufactures, and services jet and turboprop engines and integrated systems for commercial, military, business, and general aviation aircraft. The company operates through two primary reporting segments — Commercial Engines & Services (CES), which contributes roughly three-quarters of total revenue and includes the design, manufacture, and servicing of jet engines for commercial airframes (including the LEAP engine family produced through the CFM International joint venture with Safran, and widebody engines such as the GEnx and GE9X) — and Defense & Propulsion Technologies (DPT), covering military and defense-related propulsion programs including next-generation adaptive-cycle engines and collaborative combat aircraft technology. The company's brand portfolio includes Avio Aero, Unison, GE Additive, and Dowty Propellers. GE Aerospace's installed base of engines powering the global commercial fleet is the primary long-term driver of its highly profitable, multi-decade aftermarket services business.

Industry Overview & Competitive Positioning

GE Aerospace operates in the global commercial and defense aerospace engine and systems industry, an oligopolistic market dominated by a small number of major engine manufacturers (GE/CFM, RTX's Pratt & Whitney, Rolls-Royce, and Safran as GE's CFM joint-venture partner) given the extreme capital intensity, certification barriers, and multi-decade product lifecycles involved. The industry has benefited from a multi-year commercial aviation recovery and aftermarket demand surge — described by Melius Research as "several years of robust" growth — alongside sustained increases in global defense spending supporting the Defense & Propulsion Technologies segment. A persistent industry-wide constraint has been supply-chain bottlenecks limiting engine-delivery rates relative to underlying demand (evidenced by GE's own $210 billion-plus backlog and its recent CPP acquisition specifically to address a castings/precision-parts bottleneck).

Competitive Advantage ("Economic Moat")

GE Aerospace's moat is built on an enormous, multi-decade installed base of engines (particularly the ubiquitous CFM56 and its LEAP successor) that generates highly predictable, high-margin aftermarket services revenue over 10–20-year service-agreement horizons — a classic "installed base" moat common to the aerospace engine industry. The company's near-zero grounded-LEAP-aircraft milestone and continuing durability-kit rollouts (extending time-on-wing) both reinforce customer trust and reduce the risk of a competitor displacing GE on future re-engining decisions. The pending CPP acquisition extends this moat by vertically integrating a previously outsourced, bottleneck-prone input. Morningstar specifically frames the bull case as resting on operating leverage as commercial aerospace "fully recovers" and the LEAP aftermarket program "enters its profitable phase," while flagging the speculative, longer-horizon RISE open-fan engine program as a distinct source of both opportunity and technology risk for the 2040s.

Valuation Conclusion

GE Aerospace's valuation premium relative to RTX and Textron is substantial by several metrics (forward P/E, PEG, P/B), and this premium is precisely what Melius Research's fresh downgrade calls into question — arguing the market has priced in a continuation of exceptionally strong aftermarket growth that may moderate to high-single-digits next year. Countervailing this, GE's operational execution has been genuinely strong (five consecutive 20%+ revenue growth quarters, consecutive guidance raises, record backlog), and the CPP acquisition specifically addresses a supply-side constraint rather than a demand-side one.
